Flat Back

Definition and mechanism of development

Flat back is a sagittal-plane imbalance resulting from reduced lumbar lordosis. Normally, the natural curve in the lower back provides balance against gravity and regulates the load on the discs; reduction of this curve eliminates this protective mechanism.

In a flattened spine, posterior disc pressure increases, which may lead to problems such as low back pain, muscle fatigue, and compression of neural tissue. A shift of the pelvis into posterior tilt is most often associated with short hamstrings and weak hip flexors. If this postural disorder persists for a long time, additional functional disorders such as scoliosis, nerve compression, tension-type headache, and pelvic malalignment may occur.

Exercise approach

The exercise approach for flat back is based on correcting pelvic alignment and supporting lumbar lordosis.

  • Pelvic neutral-position exercises: The aim is to regain anterior pelvic tilt and bring the spine closer to its natural curve.
  • Hamstring stretching: Because shortened hamstrings increase posterior tilt, regular stretching is fundamental to the corrective effect.
  • Hip flexor strengthening: Strong flexors support forward movement of the pelvis and increase lordosis.
  • Lumbar mobilization exercises: Increasing lower-back mobility improves functional posture and movement quality.

Corrective exercise approaches (particularly Schroth-based programs) have been reported to provide the most marked improvement in lordosis angle, flexibility, and functional capacity.

Points to consider
  • Movements involving excessive forward bending: Exercises requiring excessive forward bending should be avoided because they increase posterior loading in flat back.
  • Overhead weight lifting: Lifting weights overhead while standing or sitting increases vertical load on the spine and reduces the opportunity for compensation in the region with loss of lordosis; it should be carefully limited or replaced with alternative movements.
  • Loading with tight hamstrings: Exercises such as the deadlift and good morning may disrupt posture; therefore, working on flexibility first and strength afterward is more appropriate.
  • Speed of mobility exercises: During mobility exercises, controlled mobilization accompanied by breathing should be preferred over rapid, repetitive spinal movements.
  • Monitoring form: Because the pelvis tends to shift posteriorly, form should be monitored closely during movements such as the squat, row, and deadlift; the weight should be reduced if the lower back flattens.
  • Neurological warning signs: If pulling around the leg, burning in the lower back, or an electric sensation along a nerve pathway occurs, exercise should be stopped immediately and medical evaluation should be recommended.
  • Loading strategy: In flat back, the spine compensates easily; a low-repetition, high-quality approach is more appropriate for improving neuromuscular control.
