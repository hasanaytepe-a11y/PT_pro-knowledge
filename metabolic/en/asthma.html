PT Pro – Clinical Information Note

Asthma

Definition and mechanism of development

Asthma is a respiratory disease involving chronic airway inflammation, bronchial hyperreactivity, and episodic shortness of breath, wheezing, coughing, and exercise intolerance. Exercise-induced bronchoconstriction (EIB), which may develop due to drying of the airways during exercise, is quite common and may lead to fear of exercise, avoidance, and loss of physical conditioning in some individuals.

Independently of airway obstruction, low levels of physical activity have been shown to significantly reduce exercise capacity in individuals with asthma. In contrast, regular physical activity may reduce the risk of EIB by lowering minute ventilation, reduce the perception of shortness of breath by strengthening the respiratory muscles, and improve overall quality of life. Some studies have also reported that regular exercise may reduce airway inflammation.

Exercise approach

Common features of exercise protocols for individuals with asthma:

  • Aerobic exercise: Moderate-intensity walking, running, cycling, or swimming for 20–30 minutes, 2–3 days per week.
  • Duration: At least 4 weeks; gains in VO₂max increase as total volume increases.
  • Breathing training: Diaphragmatic breathing, pursed-lip breathing.
  • Setting: Gym-based or group exercises; minimal equipment required.

Demonstrated effects:

  • Increase in VO₂max: An average of +5 ml/kg/min.
  • Increased work capacity and endurance.
  • Reduced asthma symptoms and a marked increase in the number of symptom-free days.
  • Physical and psychosocial improvement in quality of life.
  • Major changes in FEV₁, FVC, and PEF are not expected; gains relate more to tolerance and the perception of shortness of breath.
Points to consider
  • The risk of EIB is high (up to 90%): An appropriate warm-up should be performed, and cold, dry environments should be avoided.
  • A short-acting β₂-agonist may be used before exercise as recommended by a physician.
  • Safety: Aerobic exercise does not increase asthma symptoms and is well tolerated.
  • Medication use: Exercise may reduce the need for inhaled steroids in some individuals; medication adjustments should be made only by a physician.
  • Excessive loading: Performance may increase as volume increases; however, excessive volume may negatively affect perceptions of quality of life.
  • Individual adaptation: Symptom level, shortness-of-breath threshold, and level of daily control should be considered.
  • If symptoms increase, exercise should be reduced or stopped.
