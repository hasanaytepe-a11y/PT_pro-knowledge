Metabolic Syndrome

Definition and mechanism of development

Metabolic Syndrome (MetS) is a clinical condition characterized by insulin resistance and hyperinsulinemia that occurs when at least three components—such as abdominal obesity, high triglycerides, low HDL, high blood pressure, and high fasting glucose—are present together.

As a consequence of modern life, obesity, physical inactivity, high saturated/trans-fat intake, chronic stress, and hormonal–genetic factors play an important role in its development. Individuals diagnosed with MetS have a markedly increased risk of coronary artery disease and type 2 diabetes.

Exercise approach

Exercise is one of the fundamental elements in the management of MetS. The combined use of aerobic exercise and resistance training is recommended; both types of exercise are inversely associated with the prevalence of MetS.

  • Aerobic exercise: Activities such as walking, elliptical training, cycling, aquatic exercise, and pole walking may be preferred.
  • Non–weight-bearing options: Aquatic exercise or cycling, which reduces joint loading, is more appropriate for individuals with excess weight and joint problems.
  • Intensity: Individuals with poor conditioning should begin at low intensity and progress over time to moderate intensity (RPE 11–13).

The aims of the exercise program are to increase energy expenditure, reduce abdominal fat, and improve insulin sensitivity. Simple behavioral changes are encouraged to support daily movement.

  • Strategies such as taking the stairs, parking farther away, and standing up once an hour are recommended.
  • The aim is to increase the daily step count to 10,000 steps or more.
Points to consider
  • Diagnostic thresholds: Waist circumference (>102 cm for men, >88 cm for women), HDL (<40 mg/dL for men, <50 mg/dL for women), triglycerides (≥150 mg/dL), blood pressure (≥130/85 mmHg), and fasting glucose (≥110 mg/dL) are considered.
  • Pre-assessment: Because hypertension, dyslipidemia, and glycemic disorders often coexist in patients with MetS, medical assessment before exercise is important.
  • Medication use: Antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, and blood-glucose-regulating medications may affect the response to exercise and should be considered when planning the load.
  • Exercise intensity: Excessively high intensity is not recommended initially; the aim is to create sustainable energy expenditure.
  • Joint and mobility problems: In the presence of joint pain, limited mobility, or severe obesity, alternatives such as aquatic exercise or cycling should be preferred over walking.
  • Sedentary behavior: Prolonged sitting must be reduced; total daily movement volume should be regarded as one of the most critical indicators of health.
  • Long-term goals: Because weight, blood pressure, blood glucose, and lipids form an interrelated cycle, the trainer's focus should be on making long-term movement, weight control, and healthy eating habits sustainable rather than correcting a single parameter.
