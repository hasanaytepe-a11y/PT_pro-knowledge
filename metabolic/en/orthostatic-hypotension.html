Orthostatic Hypotension

Definition and mechanism of development

Orthostatic hypotension (postural hypotension) is a sudden drop in blood pressure upon standing from a lying or seated position. Clinically, it is defined as a decrease in systolic blood pressure of ≥20 mmHg and diastolic blood pressure of ≥10 mmHg within 3 minutes after a person stands following at least 5 minutes in a supine position or is brought to a 60° tilt-table position.

In addition to impairing quality of life, this condition is associated with falls, cardiovascular disease, dementia, depression, and increased mortality. Upon standing, 300–800 mL of blood pools in the lower extremities due to gravity, reducing venous return, cardiac filling, and cardiac output.

Normally, the body compensates through the baroreceptor reflex: sympathetic tone increases, blood vessels constrict, vagal tone decreases, systemic vascular resistance rises, and blood pressure is maintained. The renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) also becomes active and supports standing. In orthostatic hypotension, this response is delayed or insufficient; therefore, the drop in blood pressure is exaggerated and cerebral perfusion decreases.

Exercise approach
  • Cool-down process: The session should never be stopped abruptly; it should end with 5–10 minutes of light walking or low-paced pedaling.
  • Preferred positions: Aerobic exercises that can be performed seated or semi-reclined should be preferred over standing exercises that require prolonged static positioning.
  • Position transitions: During exercise and breaks, transitions that bring the person directly and abruptly to standing from movements performed on the floor/bench should be avoided; position changes should be planned more slowly and with control.
  • Acute maneuvers: For mild symptoms that develop during exercise, physical maneuvers such as crossing the legs, isometric contraction of the lower extremities, squatting, or bending forward may help raise blood pressure within a short time.
  • Environment and intensity: Exercise sessions should not be performed in very hot, poorly ventilated environments. Intensive standing exercises should not be planned immediately after large meals. The session pace should be moderate and controlled; a continuous, tolerable pace should be preferred over sudden peak loads.
Points to consider
  • Timing and triggers: It should be considered that orthostatic hypotension may increase, particularly in the morning, during postural changes, after exercise, in hot environments, and with dehydration.
  • Symptom monitoring: If symptoms such as dizziness, presyncope, blurred vision, imbalance, or syncope occur, exercise should be stopped immediately and the person should be placed in a safe position (seated/lying).
  • Risk of falling: Exercise sessions for individuals with a history of falls should be conducted under increased supervision.
  • Period after high intensity: The risk of presyncope may increase after high-intensity training; therefore, the cool-down should be extended and orthostatic symptoms should be monitored carefully.
  • Multidisciplinary approach: The exercise program should be planned through a holistic approach and in coordination with the team providing the individual's medical follow-up.
