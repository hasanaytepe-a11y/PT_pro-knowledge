Diabetes

Definition and clinical framework

Diabetes is a metabolic disease characterized by chronically elevated blood glucose (sugar) levels. It develops because the pancreas cannot produce enough insulin or because cells cannot respond to insulin.

Insulin is the hormone that enables glucose to be transported into cells; when the system is disrupted, glucose accumulates in the blood, and vascular, nerve, and organ damage may develop over time.

Main types:

  • Type 1 Diabetes: Destruction of insulin-producing cells through an autoimmune mechanism; requires lifelong insulin.
  • Type 2 Diabetes: Cells become insensitive to insulin (insulin resistance); this is the most common type.
  • Gestational Diabetes: Develops during pregnancy and may resolve after delivery, but the future risk of Type 2 increases.
  • Prediabetes: Blood glucose is above normal but not high enough for a diabetes diagnosis; it can be reversed with regular exercise.
Mechanism of development

Diabetes results from a combination of genetic factors, immune-system disorders, and lifestyle factors.

  • Type 1: Insulin production by the pancreas stops as a result of genetic predisposition and autoimmune processes.
  • Type 2: Inactivity, obesity, high carbohydrate intake, stress, and aging increase insulin resistance.
  • Prediabetes: Following prolonged unbalanced nutrition and low activity, cells cease to recognize insulin.

Regular exercise and healthy nutrition reduce the risk of Type 2 diabetes in particular and improve glucose control.

Exercise approach and general effects

Exercise increases insulin sensitivity, improves blood glucose control, enhances cardiovascular capacity, and contributes to weight management.

Aerobic exercise recommendations
  • Frequency: 3–7 days per week
  • Duration: 20–60 minutes daily (weekly total ≥150 minutes)
  • Intensity: Moderate (brisk walking, cycling, swimming, etc.)
  • Rule: Exercise should not be interrupted for more than two consecutive days.
Resistance (strength) exercise
  • Frequency: 2–3 days per week
  • Volume: 8–12 repetitions × 2–3 sets
  • Target: Multi-joint exercises focusing on large muscle groups
  • Combination: Aerobic and resistance training is the most effective combination for glucose control.
Points to consider
  • Blood glucose control:
    • <100 mg/dL → 20–30 g of carbohydrate should be consumed before exercise.
    • ≈250 mg/dL (ketone-positive) or >300 mg/dL (ketone-negative) → Exercise should not be performed.
  • Risk of hypoglycemia: Symptoms such as trembling, sweating, dizziness, and weakness should be monitored.
  • Insulin injection site: Insulin should preferably be injected into the abdomen, not into the muscle region being exercised.
  • Neuropathy: Care should be taken to prevent foot injuries; reduced-load, low-impact exercises (e.g., cycling) should be preferred.
  • Retinopathy: Heavy lifting and straining maneuvers that cause sudden increases in pressure should be avoided.
  • Fluid balance: Adequate fluid intake should be ensured before, during, and after exercise.
  • Environmental conditions: Exercise in hot and humid environments should be planned carefully and performed under supervision if necessary.
